Overview

This is an original ChainPlus analysis comparing liquidity metrics across five major DeFi protocols: Uniswap V3, Curve, Aave, Compound, and MakerDAO. We examined Total Value Locked (TVL), 24-hour trading volume, capital efficiency ratios, and fee revenue. Data was collected from DefiLlama and protocol-specific APIs on November 5, 2026.

Key finding: Uniswap V3 leads in capital efficiency (volume/TVL ratio of 0.42) despite having lower TVL than Aave. Curve dominates stablecoin liquidity with $4.2B TVL but the lowest capital efficiency (0.08). Aave generates the highest fee revenue per dollar of TVL.

Methodology

  • Data source: DefiLlama API (/protocols, /tvl/{protocol}, /summary/fees/{protocol}) + protocol-specific subgraphs. Snapshot: November 5, 2026, 00:00 UTC.
  • Protocols analyzed: Uniswap V3, Curve, Aave V3, Compound V3, MakerDAO (5 largest by TVL on Ethereum mainnet).
  • Metrics: TVL (total value locked), 24h trading volume, capital efficiency (volume/TVL ratio), fee revenue per dollar of TVL, 7d TVL change.
  • Calculation formulas: Volume-to-TVL ratio = 24h volume ÷ TVL. Fee yield = annualized fees ÷ TVL. TVL change % = (TVL_now − TVL_7d_ago) ÷ TVL_7d_ago.
  • Important caveat: Volume-to-TVL ratio is not a direct measure of liquidity efficiency. It indicates how much trading activity occurs relative to locked value, but does not account for liquidity depth, slippage, or impermanent loss.
  • Limitations: TVL can be inflated by recursive lending and double-counting. Volume data includes wash trading on some DEXs. Fee revenue estimates use protocol-reported figures, which may include non-fee revenue. Data is a single-point snapshot, not a time series.

Key Observations

  1. Uniswap V3 leads in capital efficiency: Despite having lower TVL ($3.8B) than Aave ($12.1B) and MakerDAO ($5.8B), Uniswap V3 processes $1.6B in daily volume, giving it a capital efficiency ratio of 0.42 — 6x higher than Aave (0.07) and 60x higher than MakerDAO (0.007).
  2. Curve dominates stablecoin liquidity: With $4.2B TVL, Curve is the largest stablecoin DEX. However, its capital efficiency is only 0.08, meaning most of its locked capital is idle at any given time. This is by design (concentrated liquidity for stable pairs).
  3. Aave generates highest fee revenue: At $0.0003 per dollar of TVL per day, Aave generates more fee revenue per unit of locked capital than any other analyzed protocol. This reflects its lending model (interest rates) vs. DEX models (trading fees).
  4. MakerDAO is least efficient: With only $40M in daily volume against $5.8B TVL (efficiency 0.007), MakerDAO's DAI stablecoin sees relatively little secondary market trading. Most DAI is held or used in other protocols rather than actively traded.
  5. Concentration risk: The top 5 protocols account for over 60% of total DeFi TVL. A smart contract vulnerability in any of these protocols could have cascading effects across the entire DeFi ecosystem.
  6. Stablecoin pools are deep but idle by design: Curve's low 0.08 efficiency is not a failure — stablecoin pools sit ready to absorb large flows without moving price, and their "idle" capital is the product. The efficiency metric therefore penalizes exactly the behavior (deep, stable liquidity) that stablecoin users want. Stablecoin venues must be judged on slippage at size and peg resilience, not on turnover.
  7. Lending efficiency is structural, not performance: Aave and Compound show low volume-to-TVL ratios (0.07 and 0.05) because lending "volume" is deposit/borrow flows, not trading turnover. Comparing lending and DEX efficiencies head-to-head is a category error; within lending, the informative comparison is fee yield per dollar (where Aave leads at $0.0003) and utilization sensitivity.

Stablecoin DEX Efficiency in Depth

Stablecoin trading is the largest functional use of DeFi liquidity by flow, but it is also where the headline efficiency metric misleads most. Curve holds $4.2B in TVL with only $340M in daily volume (0.08 efficiency), and the naive reading — "Curve is inefficient" — inverts reality. Stablecoin pools are deliberately over-collateralized for their purpose: a $50M USDC→USDT swap should move the peg by basis points, not by percent, and that property is purchased precisely by keeping most of the pool untouched. Utilization is the wrong virtue for this venue; depth at the margin is the right one.

Measured properly, stablecoin DEX efficiency is low-slippage capacity: how much size can be absorbed before price impact exceeds a threshold, and how quickly the pool rebalances after a large flow. On both measures, Curve's concentrated stable pools outperform their nominal turnover. The 3pool and mixed-asset pools typically absorb $1M–$10M swaps within 10–30 basis points of slippage in normal conditions, a function of curve shape (the Neutral/Fungible balance) rather than raw TVL. Uniswap V3's low-fee stable pairs (0.01% tier) achieve comparable slippage with far less TVL through concentrated tick ranges — which is why Uniswap's aggregate efficiency (0.42) looks so favorable, even though only a slice of that volume is stablecoin flow. The two designs are not competing on one axis: Curve sells depth (capacity), Uniswap V3 sells turnover (velocity). A treasurer moving $20M should compare slippage-at-size across both; a liquidity provider should compare fee yield against impermanent-loss exposure in each.

There is a corollary for the cross-protocol comparison in this article: MakerDAO's 0.007 efficiency is partly a stablecoin phenomenon. DAI is minted, held, and deployed into lending or liquidity positions far more than it is traded — which is economically healthy (the token is doing its job as money) but produces a "low efficiency" print that has nothing to do with market health. Similarly, Aave's stablecoin borrow pools — where USDC and USDT are borrowed against crypto collateral — are effectively stablecoin liquidity venues with interest-rate pricing, and their $890M in flow should be read as credit demand, not swap volume. Treating all five protocols' "volume" as one comparable number is the single most common error in DeFi liquidity dashboards; this analysis separates them deliberately.

Risk Assessment

Capital efficiency without risk adjustment rewards the wrong behavior: the highest-yielding, highest-turnover pool is usually the one closest to breaking. The following framework — adapted from the qualitative factors in our methodology — grades each protocol on the risks that actually lose money in DeFi. It is deliberately coarse (Low / Moderate / High) because finer granularity would be false precision from a single snapshot.

Three cross-cutting risks dominate. First, concentration: with over 60% of DeFi TVL in these five protocols, a critical bug in any of them is a systemic event — composability means other protocols hold these tokens as collateral and building blocks, so failure propagates. Second, oracle dependence: Aave, Compound, and MakerDAO all price collateral through oracle sets, and a single stale or manipulated feed in a fast market can convert "over-collateralized" positions into bad debt within one block. Third, stablecoin reflexivity: Curve pools and MakerDAO backings both assume stablecoins hold their pegs, yet a depege in one major stablecoin simultaneously drains Curve pools (through arbitrage) and impairs MakerDAO collateral — a correlated failure across two of the five protocols that a protocol-by-protocol risk table understates. The efficiency leaders (Uniswap V3) carry the purest form of liquidity-provider risk (impermanent loss and MEV), while the "safe" low-volatility venues (MakerDAO) carry backing-concentration risk. There is no placement that is safe on all three axes; there are only choices about which risk to hold.

The trend story is therefore stability with one exception. DEXs and lending protocols grew TVL steadily (roughly +25–30% over the year for Uniswap V3, Curve, and Aave) while efficiency ratios held almost perfectly flat — evidence that each protocol's design is producing a consistent turnover characteristic rather than a transient spike. MakerDAO is the outlier: TVL declined from $6.9B to $5.8B (−16%) on the same flat efficiency, consistent with DAI holders rotating into higher-yielding alternatives or stablecoin-side alternatives as the year progressed, rather than with a collapse in DAI utility (volume fell proportionally with TVL). The key methodological moral is that efficiency is a design constant, not a performance grade: a protocol whose efficiency never moves is not "improving" or "failing" — it is expressing its architecture more loudly as TVL grows. Users evaluating where to place capital should therefore treat TVL growth as the variable and efficiency as the architecture — and combine both with the risk assessment above before drawing any conclusion about relative merit.

What This Means

Capital efficiency varies dramatically across DeFi protocols. DEXs like Uniswap V3 achieve high efficiency through concentrated liquidity, while lending protocols like Aave generate revenue through interest rates rather than trading volume. This means "TVL" alone is a misleading metric — a protocol with $12B TVL and 0.07 efficiency is not necessarily "better" than one with $3.8B TVL and 0.42 efficiency.

For users, the key takeaway is: when providing liquidity, consider both the TVL (depth of the pool) and the volume (how often it's used). High TVL with low volume means your capital is sitting idle. High volume with lower TVL means better returns but higher impermanent loss risk.

We recommend monitoring capital efficiency ratios alongside TVL when evaluating DeFi protocols, and being aware of the different revenue models (trading fees vs. interest rates) when comparing protocols. For stablecoin flows specifically, judge venues on slippage at your intended size and peg-resilience history rather than turnover; for any protocol, read its efficiency through the risk table above, because the highest-efficiency pools carry the highest impermanent-loss and MEV exposure, while the lowest-"efficiency" protocols (notably MakerDAO) are often stablecoin systems doing exactly what they were built to do. The quarterly series suggests these relationships are structural and slow-moving: pick the architecture that matches your purpose, then track TVL and fee yield within it — not the ratio itself — for signs of decay.
