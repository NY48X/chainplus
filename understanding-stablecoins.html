Understanding Stablecoins: USDT, USDC, and Beyond

By Mike Tembo · September 11, 2026 · Explainer

Stablecoins are cryptocurrencies designed to stay at one dollar. That single design goal quietly underpins almost everything in crypto: trading, lending, payments and remittances all need a unit that does not swing 10% overnight. Remove the volatility and you remove the biggest obstacle to using blockchains for real money.

The short version — in plain English

  • What they are: Tokens pegged to $1, backed in different ways — by cash and Treasuries, by crypto collateral, or by algorithms (dangerously). The backing is the whole story.
  • The big three: USDT (Tether) has the deepest liquidity, USDC (Circle) the strongest transparency and compliance, and DAI (now USDS) the most decentralised design.
  • The lesson of the last decade: A peg is a promise, not a law of physics. Every stablecoin, including well-backed ones, has depegged at least briefly when confidence broke.

Why Stablecoins Matter

Without stablecoins, every crypto transaction carried exchange-rate risk. If you wanted to move money from one exchange to another, lend it out, or send it to family abroad, you either used a volatile token or went back through a bank. Stablecoins close that gap: a dollar on a blockchain that settles in minutes and costs cents to move.

They are also the plumbing of DeFi. Most lending markets, exchanges and yield strategies are denominated in stablecoins because no sane lender wants their loan repaid in an asset that could fall 20% before the loan is due. And they are the largest holders of US Treasury bills among non-sovereign entities — a fact that increasingly matters to regulators and to the dollar itself.

How We Got Here: A Short History With Dates

October 2014 — Tether (USDT) launches. Founded as Realcoin by a group including Bitfinex, Tether promised each token was backed by real dollars. That promise, and years of disputes about what actually sits in the reserves, defined the stablecoin debate ever since.

December 2017 — MakerDAO launches DAI. The first major crypto-collateralised stablecoin. Users locked Ethereum into contracts and minted DAI against it, over-collateralised to survive price swings. It proved a stablecoin could exist with no bank involved.

September 2018 — Circle launches USDC. Backed by Coinbase and Goldman Sachs, USDC competed on transparency and regulation from day one. That positioning made it the default choice for institutions and DeFi protocols that want an auditable dollar.

May 2022 — the Terra UST collapse. Terra's algorithmic stablecoin, which maintained its peg through a mint-and-burn relationship with the LUNA token, unravelled in days and erased over $40 billion. It remains the largest failure in crypto history and the reason "algorithmic" now carries a warning label.

March 2023 — USDC depegs to $0.87. When Silicon Valley Bank failed, Circle disclosed that part of its reserves were stuck there. USDC fell sharply for days before recovering. Even the most conservative stablecoin is only as safe as the banks holding its reserves.

2024 – 2026 — regulation arrives. The EU's MiCA framework and successive US proposals moved stablecoins into a compliance regime: authorised issuers, eligible reserves, regular attestations. Scale now favours the issuers who can afford to comply.

Three Designs, Three Kinds of Risk

Fiat-backed (USDT, USDC). For every token, the issuer holds a dollar's worth of cash and short-term government securities. The risk is not the design but the audit: do the reserves exist, are they what is claimed, and are they reachable in a crisis? USDC publishes monthly attestations; Tether's history of partial disclosures is why many DeFi protocols prefer USDC.

Crypto-backed (DAI / USDS). Users deposit crypto into contracts and mint stablecoins against it, typically at 130–150% collateral. The system is over-collateralised so that even sharp drops should not break the peg. The risk is complexity: smart contract bugs and cascading liquidations in extreme markets.

Algorithmic (UST and successors). No reserves, only code balancing supply against demand. When confidence held, the peg held. When it broke in May 2022, the balancing mechanism accelerated the collapse. The consensus since then is that algorithmic stability without reserves is not stability at all.

Why It Matters

Stablecoins are where crypto meets the traditional financial system. They move real dollars, hold real Treasuries, and are now subject to real regulation in major jurisdictions. For users in countries with inflation or capital controls, a stablecoin in a self-custody wallet is often the most practical financial tool crypto has produced.

For the industry, the implication is that the most important competition is no longer stablecoin versus stablecoin — it is private stablecoins versus central bank digital currencies, and compliant issuers versus offshore ones. The winners will be decided as much in legislatures and audits as in code.

How to Read a Stablecoin Reserve Report

If you hold a fiat-backed stablecoin in size, the reserve report is the document that matters most. A good report answers four questions plainly. First, what exactly is held — cash, bank deposits, Treasury bills, commercial paper, or something riskier — and in what proportions? Second, who holds it, and are those custodians insured, regulated and independent of the issuer? Third, how often is it checked — monthly attestations by an accounting firm are the baseline, and "trust us" is not an attestation. Fourth, what happens in a crisis: can reserves be redeemed at par quickly, or are they locked in instruments that would need to be sold at a loss?

The same discipline applies to crypto-backed designs, with different questions. What is the collateral composition, how quickly are liquidations executed when prices fall, and what share of the collateral is itself a stablecoin or other complex asset? DAI and its successor USDS have published these parameters for years, which is why decentralised stablecoins are easier to evaluate than their marketing suggests — the numbers are on-chain.

Two habits follow from this. Diversify across at least two backing models if you hold large balances, so that a failure in one design does not take out all of your dollar exposure. And treat any stablecoin that cannot answer the four questions above in one page as a trading tool at best, not as a place to park savings. The peg is only as good as the evidence behind it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which stablecoin is safest?

On transparency and regulation, USDC. On liquidity, USDT. On decentralisation, DAI/USDS. 'Safest' depends on which risk you fear most — reserve opacity, bank exposure, or smart contract failure — which is why holding one type and understanding its specific risk matters more than picking a winner.

Can stablecoins lose their peg?

Yes, and most major ones have, at least briefly. USDC fell to $0.87 in March 2023. The size and duration of a depeg depends on how quickly confidence returns, which depends on how credible the reserves are.

Are stablecoins an investment?

They are a tool, not an investment. They do not grow, so holding them long-term loses purchasing power to inflation. Their value is stability and speed, not return.

What is the difference between USDT and USDC?

USDT has more liquidity and broader exchange acceptance but a less transparent reserve history. USDC is more regulated and auditable, which is why DeFi protocols and institutions generally prefer it. Both aim to be one dollar; they differ in how much you have to trust the issuer.
