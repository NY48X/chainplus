How to Spot Crypto Scams and Rug Pulls

By ChainPlus Team · October 20, 2026 · Security

Crypto scams are everywhere. From rug pulls to phishing attacks, the space is full of people trying to separate you from your money. Here's how to spot the most common scams and protect yourself.

Rug Pulls

A rug pull occurs when a project's developers drain the liquidity pool and abandon the project, leaving token holders with worthless assets. This is most common in DeFi and memecoin projects.

Red flags:

  • Anonymous team with no track record
  • No locked liquidity (developers can withdraw at any time)
  • Unaudited smart contracts
  • Excessive marketing and hype with no substance
  • Token distribution heavily skewed toward insiders

Pump and Dumps

A group accumulates a low-cap token, hypes it on social media, and sells when the price peaks. Late buyers are left holding the bag.

Red flags:

  • Sudden price spikes with no news or fundamentals
  • Coordinated social media campaigns
  • Low liquidity (easy to manipulate price)
  • Unknown or anonymous promoters

Phishing Attacks

Fake websites, emails, or social media accounts that impersonate legitimate services. They trick users into entering seed phrases or connecting wallets.

Red flags:

  • URLs that look slightly different from the real site (e.g., "uniswap.com" vs "uniswap.org")
  • Urgent messages asking you to "verify" your wallet
  • Requests for your seed phrase (no legitimate service will ever ask)
  • Too-good-to-be-true offers (airdrops, giveaways)

Fake Projects

Projects that look legitimate but are designed to steal funds. They may have professional websites, whitepapers, and social media presence.

Red flags:

  • Copied whitepaper or code from other projects
  • No working product or prototype
  • Unrealistic promises (guaranteed returns, "risk-free" investments)
  • Pressure to invest quickly ("limited time offer")

Protection Checklist

  1. Research the team. Are they doxxed? Do they have a track record?
  2. Check the code. Is it audited? Is it open source?
  3. Verify liquidity. Is it locked? For how long?
  4. Check token distribution. Are insiders holding too much?
  5. Start small. Never invest more than you can afford to lose.
  6. Use established platforms. Stick to well-known DEXs and protocols.

The Golden Rule

If something seems too good to be true, it probably is. Crypto is full of opportunities, but it's also full of people trying to exploit your greed and fear. When in doubt, don't invest.

Due Diligence Framework

Before investing in any crypto project, run through this checklist:

  1. Team: Are they doxxed? Do they have LinkedIn profiles? Have they worked on previous projects?
  2. Code: Is it open source? Has it been audited? By whom?
  3. Tokenomics: What's the total supply? How is it distributed? Is there a vesting schedule?
  4. Liquidity: Is it locked? For how long? What's the lock contract address?
  5. Community: Is the community organic or bot-driven? Are discussions genuine?
  6. Documentation: Is there a clear whitepaper? Does it explain the technology and tokenomics?
  7. Track record: Has the team delivered on previous promises? Are there red flags in their history?

Resources for Verification

  • Token Sniffer: Automated scam detection for new tokens.
  • RugDoc: Reviews DeFi projects for rug pull risk.
  • CertiK: Smart contract audits and security scores.
  • Etherscan/BscScan: Verify contract code and token distribution.
  • DexTools: Check liquidity, holder distribution, and trading activity.

Real-World Examples

Squid Game Token (2021): A token themed around the Netflix show rose 75,000% in days before developers drained $3.3 million in liquidity. The token had anti-dump mechanisms that prevented selling — a red flag that many missed.

FTX Collapse (2022): Not a traditional scam but a massive fraud. FTX misused customer funds, leading to an $8 billion shortfall. The lesson: even large, seemingly legitimate platforms can fail. Don't keep more on an exchange than you can afford to lose.

OneCoin (2014-2017): A $4 billion Ponzi scheme disguised as a cryptocurrency. It had no blockchain — just a centralized database. The founder disappeared and was never caught.

Reporting Scams

If you encounter a scam:

  • Report to the platform where it occurred (exchange, DEX, social media).
  • Report to relevant authorities (FTC, SEC, local law enforcement).
  • Warn others in crypto communities.
  • Document everything (screenshots, transaction hashes, addresses).

While recovery is rare, reporting helps protect others and may contribute to law enforcement action.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I verify if a token is legitimate?

Check: Is the contract verified on Etherscan/BscScan? Is liquidity locked? What's the token distribution? Is the team doxxed? Are there audits? Use tools like Token Sniffer and RugDoc for automated analysis. When in doubt, don't invest.

What should I do if I've been scammed?

Document everything (transaction hashes, addresses, screenshots). Report to the platform and relevant authorities. Warn others in crypto communities. Recovery is rare but reporting helps law enforcement and protects others.

Are all new tokens scams?

No, but most fail. The vast majority of new tokens lose value rapidly. Some are legitimate projects with real utility. The challenge is distinguishing legitimate projects from scams. Due diligence is essential.

How can I protect myself from phishing?

Bookmark official sites and use those instead of searching. Check URLs carefully. Never click links in unsolicited messages. Use a hardware wallet for significant holdings. Enable 2FA on all accounts. Be skeptical of too-good-to-be-true offers.
