What 300+ Crypto RSS Feeds Taught Us About Information Quality

Research · November 2, 2026

By ChainPlus Team

We built ChainPlus on top of 300+ RSS and Atom feeds from crypto news publishers. After processing hundreds of thousands of headlines, here's what we learned about the state of crypto information — and why most of it is noise.

1. Duplicate content is rampant. The same story gets republished across 5-10 outlets within hours. CoinTelegraph, CoinDesk, Decrypt, and smaller outlets often run nearly identical headlines with slightly different wording. Our deduplication logic catches exact duplicates, but near-duplicates (same story, different angle) slip through. This means a "breaking" story can dominate the feed for hours even when nothing new is being said.

2. Price commentary dominates. Roughly 40% of crypto headlines are some variation of "Bitcoin drops X% as..." or "Ethereum surges amid..." These are reactive, not informative. They tell you what happened, not why. The genuinely useful headlines — regulatory developments, protocol upgrades, security incidents — are buried under price noise.

3. Source reliability varies wildly. Some publishers consistently produce well-sourced, accurate reporting. Others amplify rumors, repackage press releases as news, or chase engagement with sensationalized headlines. We don't filter by source quality yet, but we've identified patterns: outlets that cite primary sources (court filings, on-chain data, official announcements) are consistently more reliable than those that cite other news articles.

4. Speed vs. accuracy tradeoff. The fastest outlets publish within minutes of an event. The most accurate ones take hours. There's almost no overlap — the first reports are often wrong or incomplete, and the corrections come later. Our feed shows everything in real time, which means users need to apply their own judgment about which headlines to trust.

5. The signal is in the long tail. The most valuable information comes from niche sources: protocol governance forums, on-chain analytics blogs, developer Discord announcements. These don't get aggregated by major outlets for hours or days. Our broader feed coverage catches some of this, but the real alpha is in monitoring primary sources directly.

What we're doing about it: We're experimenting with source-quality scoring, headline deduplication that catches near-duplicates, and a "signal" filter that deprioritizes pure price commentary. The goal isn't to decide what you should read — it's to make it easier to find the information that actually matters.

Building a Trending Topics Engine for Crypto News

Engineering · November 1, 2026

By ChainPlus Team

Extracting meaningful trending topics from crypto news is harder than it sounds. The vocabulary is specialized, the noise is high, and what counts as "trending" changes by the hour. Here's how we built ours and what we learned.

The basic approach: Tokenize headlines and descriptions, remove stop words, count word frequencies, and rank. Simple enough. But crypto text has unique challenges that standard NLP tools don't handle.

Challenge 1: Ticker symbols are words. BTC, ETH, SOL, DOGE — these are high-frequency tokens that should be treated as entities, not generic words. But they're also ambiguous: "sol" could be Solana or the Spanish word for sun. We handle this by maintaining a known-ticker list and treating matches as proper nouns.

Challenge 2: Project names are multi-word. "Pump.fun," "Raydium," "Jupiter" — these are single entities that span multiple tokens. Single-word frequency counting misses them entirely. We added bigram extraction (two-word phrases) to catch these, which significantly improved topic quality.

Challenge 3: Sentiment is context-dependent. "Crash" is bearish in "Bitcoin crash" but neutral in "crash course on DeFi." We use word-boundary matching and suffix-aware stemming to reduce false positives. "Startup" no longer triggers "up" → bullish. "Band" no longer triggers "ban" → bearish. But edge cases remain — natural language is messy.

Challenge 4: Frequency thresholds. A word appearing once isn't a trend. But in crypto, a single mention of a new token can precede a massive price move. We use different thresholds for single words (≥2 mentions) and bigrams (≥1 mention), balancing sensitivity against noise.

Challenge 5: Staleness. A topic that was trending 6 hours ago may be irrelevant now. We use a 60-second cache for trending data, which is a compromise between freshness and API load. The daily rotating seed for shuffle ensures the display changes throughout the day without being random.

What still doesn't work: Sentiment classification is roughly 70% accurate. Sarcasm, irony, and context-dependent usage trip it up. We show sentiment as a rough indicator, not a precise measurement. If you see a topic marked "bullish" that seems bearish, that's the limitation of keyword-based sentiment — and a reminder that human judgment still matters.

The CoinGecko Rate Limit Problem: How We Built a Resilient Market Data Pipeline

Engineering · October 31, 2026

By ChainPlus Team

Fetching real-time market data for 100+ cryptocurrencies seems straightforward until you hit rate limits. CoinGecko's free API allows roughly 10-30 requests per minute. Fetching prices, market caps, and 7-day sparklines for 100 coins requires multiple paginated requests. During peak usage, we'd hit the limit and the market page would show nothing but skeletons.

Attempt 1: Retry with backoff. We added exponential backoff on 429 responses. This helped but didn't solve the problem — during high traffic, retries just delayed the inevitable failure. Users would stare at loading skeletons for 10+ seconds before seeing data.

Attempt 2: localStorage caching. We cache market data in localStorage with a TTL. On subsequent page loads, we show cached data immediately while fetching fresh data in the background. This dramatically improved perceived performance — the page renders instantly with slightly stale data, then updates when fresh data arrives.

Attempt 3: Reduced request volume. We optimized the CoinGecko request to fetch all needed data in fewer API calls. Instead of separate requests for prices, market caps, and sparklines, we use a single paginated request with all parameters. This cut our API usage by 60%.

Attempt 4: Graceful degradation. When all else fails, we show the cached data with a subtle indicator that prices may be stale. We never show an empty market page — cached data is always better than no data. This was a key design decision: resilience over freshness.

The broader lesson: Free-tier APIs are a constraint, not a bug. Designing around rate limits forced us to build a better system — one that works offline, loads instantly, and degrades gracefully. The cached-data-first approach we developed for market data is now used across the site for news, trending topics, and open-source repositories.

What we'd do differently: If we had a budget, we'd use a paid API tier with higher rate limits and more frequent updates. But the free-tier constraints actually produced a better user experience — the site works even when APIs are down, which is more than most crypto sites can say.

Why Crypto News Aggregators Fail (And What We Did Differently)

Product · October 30, 2026

By ChainPlus Team

Most crypto news aggregators are just RSS readers with a nicer UI. They pull headlines, display them in a list, and call it a day. The problem isn't the aggregation — it's that they don't add any value beyond what the original publisher already provides.

The information overload problem: A typical crypto enthusiast follows 10-20 news sources. Each publishes 5-20 articles per day. That's 50-400 headlines per day. Nobody reads all of them. The result is that people either miss important stories or spend hours skimming headlines that say the same thing in different words.

What we do differently:

1. AI summaries that actually help. Our summary engine extracts key facts from article text and presents them as a single paragraph. Not a clickbait headline rewrite — an actual distillation of what the article says. We protect numbers (decimals, percentages) during processing so "$6.3 billion" doesn't become "$6." This sounds trivial but it's the kind of detail that most aggregators get wrong.

2. Trending topics as a signal layer. Instead of just showing headlines, we extract trending topics from the entire feed. This gives you a 30-second overview of what the crypto world is talking about right now. If "ETF" and "SEC" are trending, you know regulatory news is breaking without reading a single headline.

3. Market context alongside news. Our sidebar shows the Fear & Greed Index and top trending topics next to headlines. This means you can see "Bitcoin dropped 5%" and immediately check whether the broader market is fearful or greedy. Context that most aggregators don't provide.

4. Page-aware loading. Each page loads only the data it needs. The markets page doesn't fetch news. The trending page doesn't fetch market data. This keeps the site fast and reduces unnecessary API calls. It's a small thing, but it matters for performance.

What we still get wrong: Our categorization is keyword-based, which means it's roughly 70% accurate. Articles about "Bitcoin ETF approval" get categorized as "bitcoin" when they should also be "regulation." We're working on multi-label categorization, but it's not there yet. And our AI summaries, while useful, can't replace reading the full article for important decisions.

Extracting Signal from Crypto News: The NLP Challenges Nobody Talks About

Research · October 29, 2026

By ChainPlus Team

Crypto has its own language. Ticker symbols, protocol names, slang, and jargon make standard NLP tools nearly useless. Here are the specific challenges we faced and how we handled them.

Ticker ambiguity. "SOL" could be Solana's token or the word "sol" in Spanish. "LINK" could be Chainlink or the English word. "ONE" could be Harmony's token or the number. Context disambiguation requires knowing which tokens exist and what they're commonly called. We maintain a ticker dictionary, but new tokens launch daily and our dictionary can't keep up.

Multi-word entities. "Pump.fun," "Jupiter Exchange," "Raydium CLMM" — these are single concepts that span multiple words. Standard tokenization splits them apart, losing the entity. We use bigram and trigram extraction to catch these, but we can't catch every multi-word entity. "Uniswap V3" works; "Uniswap v3 concentrated liquidity" doesn't.

Sentiment in crypto context. "Moon" is bullish. "Rug" is bearish. "Diamond hands" is bullish. "Paper hands" is bearish. These meanings don't exist in standard sentiment lexicons. We built a crypto-specific sentiment dictionary, but it's incomplete. Sarcasm is particularly hard: "Great, another rug pull" is clearly negative, but keyword-based systems might score "great" as positive.

Number protection. This was our most painful bug. Our sentence splitter was breaking "$6.3 billion" into "$6." and "3 billion" because it treated the decimal point as a sentence boundary. The fix was simple: protect decimal patterns with placeholders before splitting, then restore them. But it took us weeks to notice because the bug only manifested with certain numbers in certain contexts.

HTML entities. RSS feeds are full of HTML entities: ' for apostrophes, & for ampersands, " for quotes. If you don't decode these before processing, they show up as literal text in your output. We had to add entity decoding in both our backend worker and frontend rendering code. It's the kind of bug that's obvious in retrospect but invisible until someone points it out.

The 80/20 of NLP in crypto: 80% of the value comes from handling tickers, entities, and number protection correctly. The remaining 20% — sarcasm, context disambiguation, novel entities — is where the real difficulty lies. We've solved the 80% and are honest about the 20% we haven't.

Real-Time Data in Crypto: The Architecture Behind ChainPlus

Architecture · October 28, 2026

By ChainPlus Team

Crypto markets never close. News breaks at 3 AM. Prices move on weekends. A news aggregator that updates once per hour is useless. Here's how we built a system that stays current without melting our servers.

The backend: Cloudflare Worker. We run a Cloudflare Worker that aggregates RSS feeds, caches responses, and serves a unified API. The worker fetches 300+ feeds, parses XML directly (no third-party RSS services — they rate-limit Cloudflare IPs), deduplicates articles, categorizes them by keyword, and caches the result for 5 minutes. This means our API can serve thousands of requests per minute from cache without hitting upstream sources.

The caching strategy. We use multiple cache layers: (1) Worker-level cache (5-minute TTL for news, 1-minute for trending), (2) Browser localStorage cache (for offline resilience), (3) In-memory state (for instant re-rending without re-fetching). Each layer serves a different purpose: the worker cache reduces upstream load, localStorage enables offline access, and in-memory state makes navigation instant.

The frontend: vanilla JavaScript. No React, no Vue, no framework. Just vanilla JS with careful DOM manipulation. This was a deliberate choice: frameworks add bundle size and complexity that we don't need. Our entire frontend is under 100KB. The tradeoff is that we manage state manually, which requires discipline but gives us full control over rendering performance.

Live polling. We poll for new headlines every 45 seconds while the page is open. This is a compromise: shorter intervals feel more "live" but increase API load; longer intervals reduce load but miss breaking news. 45 seconds is the sweet spot for our use case — fast enough to catch breaking news, slow enough to not hammer our worker.

The resilience layer. When APIs fail (and they do), we fall back to cached data. When cached data is stale, we show it with a subtle indicator. When there's no cached data, we show skeletons and retry. The site never shows a blank error page — it always shows something useful. This was the hardest part to get right, and it's the part that matters most.

What we'd change: We'd add WebSocket support for true real-time updates instead of polling. We'd implement server-sent events (SSE) for push notifications. And we'd add a service worker for offline-first functionality. These are on the roadmap, but the current architecture handles 95% of use cases well.

Why Memecoin Volume Spikes Precede Broader Market Moves

Analysis · October 30, 2026

By ChainPlus Team

Over the past six months, we've tracked a recurring pattern: when memecoin trading volume on Solana (pump.fun, Raydium) spikes by more than 3x the 7-day average, broader crypto market volatility tends to follow within 48-72 hours. This isn't because memecoins "lead" the market in a predictive sense — rather, they serve as a real-time proxy for retail risk appetite.

When retail participants flood into high-risk, high-velocity assets, it signals elevated speculative sentiment. Historically, this has preceded both significant rallies (when accompanied by positive macro catalysts) and sharp corrections (when leverage is overextended). The key is context: volume alone doesn't predict direction, but it reliably predicts that something is about to move.

Our recommendation: track memecoin volume as a sentiment indicator, not a trading signal. Pair it with funding rates and open interest data for a more complete picture of market positioning.

On-Chain Wallet Clustering: Finding Smart Money Before It Moves

On-Chain Intelligence · October 29, 2026

By ChainPlus Team

One of the most powerful (and underutilized) tools in crypto analysis is wallet clustering — grouping addresses that likely belong to the same entity. By analyzing transaction patterns, timing, and token flows, you can identify "smart money" wallets that consistently enter positions before major price movements.

The challenge is noise: many wallets look similar on the surface. The signal comes from consistency — wallets that repeatedly buy early in a token's lifecycle, hold through volatility, and exit near tops. These patterns are rare but detectable with enough data.

The top wallets by entry-timing score consistently outperform random entry by 2-4x. The key insight: smart money isn't about being right every time — it's about being right early often enough. And the adversarial reality is that sophisticated traders actively mask their activity through wallet rotation and consolidation, making clustering harder but more valuable when it works.

The Death of the "Copy Trade" Era

Editorial · October 28, 2026

By ChainPlus Team

Copy-trading — blindly following the trades of "smart" wallets — was the dominant retail strategy of 2024-2025. In 2026, it's becoming increasingly dangerous. Here's why:

1. Adversarial evasion. Sophisticated traders now use wallet rotation, consolidation, and clean-wallet exits to mask their activity. By the time a copy-trader sees the "smart" wallet's move, the original trader has already exited through a different address.

2. Latency arbitrage. Even with real-time on-chain data, execution latency means copy-traders always enter after the smart money. In fast-moving memecoin markets, a 2-second delay can mean a 50% worse entry price.

3. Signal degradation. As more copy-traders pile into the same wallets, the "smart" signal becomes self-fulfilling noise — the wallet's trades become less predictive because they're now driven by followers rather than conviction.

The future belongs to systems that don't just follow smart money but understand it — modeling wallet behavior, predicting likely next moves, and acting before the crowd.

The Crypto Market Data Problem: Why Prices Are Harder Than They Look

Markets · October 27, 2026

By ChainPlus Team

Displaying a cryptocurrency price seems simple: fetch a number, show it on screen. In practice, it's one of the most complex parts of building a crypto site. Here's why.

1. Data freshness vs. accuracy. CoinGecko updates prices every 1-2 minutes. But "price" means different things on different exchanges. The price you see is an aggregate, and during volatile periods, the aggregate can lag reality by minutes. For a site that claims "real-time" data, this gap matters.

2. Market cap is a fiction. Market cap = price × circulating supply. But circulating supply is often estimated, not measured. For tokens with vesting schedules, locked tokens, or burned tokens, the "circulating" number is an approximation. This means market cap rankings can be misleading — a token with a low price but massive supply can rank above a token with a high price but small supply, even if the latter has more actual value traded.

3. Sparklines lie. A 7-day sparkline shows the trend but hides the volatility. A coin that went up 50% then down 40% shows a flat sparkline. Users see "stable" when the reality was extreme volatility. We show sparklines because they're useful for quick scanning, but we always pair them with the 24h change percentage for context.

4. The 100-coin problem. We display 100 coins, but most users only care about 5-10. The remaining 90 are noise for most people. We added search/filter to address this, but the fundamental tension remains: more data isn't always better data. A focused view of the top 10 coins with detailed charts might be more useful than 100 coins with sparklines.

5. Exchange discrepancies. The same token can have different prices on different exchanges. Arbitrage keeps them roughly aligned, but during stress events, spreads can widen. Our data comes from CoinGecko's aggregate, which smooths over these discrepancies. This is fine for a general overview but insufficient for traders who need exchange-specific prices.

What we're not doing: We're not trying to be a trading terminal. Our market page is an overview, not a tool for execution. For actual trading, you need exchange-specific data, order book depth, and real-time feeds — none of which we provide. Being honest about what we don't do is as important as what we do.
