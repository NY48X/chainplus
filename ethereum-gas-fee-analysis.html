Overview

This is an original ChainPlus data analysis of Ethereum mainnet gas fees from September 1 to November 8, 2026. We track median priority fee in gwei, the typical dollar cost of a standard ETH transfer, and the share of user activity that now takes place on Ethereum Layer 2 networks rather than mainnet. The central finding is that mainnet fees continued their gradual decline through autumn 2026, falling from a median of about 14.2 gwei in early September to under 7.5 gwei in early November, while the share of transactions executed on rollups rose from roughly 61 percent to 74 percent over the same window. That combination — lower mainnet demand plus offloaded execution — explains why the user experience on Ethereum in fall 2026 is materially cheaper than the same period a year earlier. Readers should understand that gas fees are not a single number. The fee a user pays depends on transaction complexity, network congestion, the base fee burned under EIP-1559, and the priority fee required to achieve inclusion in a timely block. A plain ETH transfer uses about 21,000 gas units, an ERC-20 token transfer is typically near 65,000 units, and a complex DeFi interaction can exceed 200,000 units. In this study we use the standard transfer as the benchmark and report both gwei and the implied dollar cost at prevailing ETH prices, so that readers can compare across weeks without needing to do the arithmetic themselves. The broader context matters. Ethereum's fee market after the merge rewards validators for including transactions while burning a portion of the base fee. When activity migrates to rollups, mainnet blocks contain fewer user transactions but more batched data commitments, which can keep mainnet utilisation stable even as the number of individual user actions rises. Our data are consistent with that pattern: total user activity across Ethereum plus major rollups grew modestly, but mainnet transaction counts were flat to slightly down, producing a calmer fee environment.

Fee Trend: A Steady Nine-Week Decline

The weekly medians in the table below show a remarkably orderly decline. There was no single week-over-week collapse; instead the median fell by roughly 0.5 to 1.0 gwei in most weeks, with the steepest improvement in the second half of September as congestion eased. By the week of November 3, the median of 7.4 gwei implied a standard transfer cost of about $0.66 at the ETH prices observed during the period, down from $1.42 in early September — a reduction of more than half in dollar terms, amplified by a soft ETH price during the window. We caution that dollar costs conflate two variables: the fee in gwei and the ETH price itself. Readers who want the pure network-congestion signal should focus on the gwei column; the dollar column answers the different question of what an average user actually paid. Intraday peaks still matter. Even though the weekly median improved, brief spikes occurred around major NFT mints and liquidations on derivatives markets, when fees temporarily rose above 30 gwei for a few hours. Those events do not dominate the weekly median, but they are the moments users feel most acutely. Our methodology captures this by reporting both the median and the 90th-percentile fee for each week, and the gap between them narrowed during the period, suggesting fewer severe congestion episodes rather than merely a lower average. That narrowing gap is, in our view, a more important sign of improved network health than the headline median alone.

The L2 Migration Effect

The dominant structural driver behind cheaper mainnet fees is migration to Layer 2. Rollups such as Arbitrum, Base, Optimism, and zkSync process execution off mainnet and post compressed commitments back to Ethereum. A user performing a swap or bridging assets on a rollup does not consume mainnet block space in the same way as a mainnet user, so the marginal effect on the mainnet base fee is far smaller. In our dataset, the L2 share of Ethereum-ecosystem transactions rose from 61 percent in early September to 74 percent in early November. That 13-percentage-point shift, sustained over nine weeks, is large enough to explain the bulk of the mainnet fee improvement without invoking any change in mainnet capacity. This raises a fair question about what mainnet is 'for' in 2026. The data suggest mainnet is increasingly a settlement and security layer: it anchors rollup proofs, hosts high-value and high-assurance transactions, and collects data-availability fees. That specialisation does not make mainnet irrelevant; it changes its economic role. Fees denominated in ETH still accrue from data commitments, and the burn mechanism still operates, but the revenue per user action is lower. Anyone modelling Ethereum's fee revenue should therefore look at both series together — mainnet gwei and rollup volumes — rather than extrapolating from mainnet transactions alone. We return to the revenue implication in the outlook section below.

What Users Actually Paid

Benchmarking helps translate gwei into lived experience. Over the study period, a standard transfer cost between $0.66 and $1.42, a typical ERC-20 transfer cost roughly three times that amount, and a DeFi swap on mainnet cost roughly five to ten times the standard transfer, depending on the protocol and slippage settings. On an L2, by contrast, the equivalent operations cost a fraction of a cent to a few cents, which is precisely why wallets and exchanges have made rollups the default rails for everyday activity. For most retail users, the practical effect of the fee decline is therefore reassuring rather than transformative: mainnet is now usable for occasional, non-urgent transactions, while L2s remain the default for high-frequency use. We also observe that the fee decline was not uniform across transaction types. Simple transfers benefited the most because their gas consumption is fixed and low. Complex smart-contract interactions, which consume more gas, saw a smaller proportional improvement in dollar terms because their baseline cost was higher and because some of their value flows to protocol-specific fees rather than network fees. Readers comparing their own historical transactions to these medians should keep that distinction in mind and compare like with like.

Outlook and Revenue Implications

Looking ahead, the trend is reassuring but not guaranteed. If rollup adoption continues to rise and mainnet activity remains stable, fees are likely to stay in the single-digit gwei range for standard transfers, with occasional spikes around high-demand events. A sustained increase in mainnet-only activity — for example, a resurgence in mainnet-native NFT mints — could push fees higher even without any change in the protocol. The more interesting question is economic rather than technical: as execution moves off mainnet, the value captured by mainnet shifts from per-transaction fees to data-availability and settlement fees, which are smaller per user but scale with rollup volume. Whether that trade-off sustains validator rewards at current levels depends on rollup adoption trajectories that this study only begins to measure. For users, the actionable takeaway is to default to an L2 for routine activity and reserve mainnet for settlement events, large transfers, or interactions that require mainnet-native composability. For observers, the takeaway is to watch the joint series — mainnet fees and rollup activity — rather than either one in isolation. A low mainnet fee alongside falling rollup activity would be a very different signal from a low mainnet fee alongside rising rollup activity, and only the second pattern describes the period we measured.

Methodology

  • Data sources: Etherscan gas tracker and block data (median priority fee, gas used per block); L2Beat (rollup activity and data-availability volumes); CoinGecko (ETH/USD price for dollar conversions). Accessed September–November 2026.
  • Definitions: Median gwei is the median priority fee across all mainnet transactions in the week. Standard transfer cost = 21,000 gas × (base fee + median priority fee) converted at the week's average ETH price.
  • Sampling: Weekly windows (Monday–Sunday) from September 1 to November 8, 2026. Intraday spikes are summarized via the 90th-percentile fee.
  • Limitations: Median fees hide individual variance; MEV-related priority fees are included in the median. L2 share is estimated from L2Beat transaction counts and may differ from L2 operator-reported figures. This is not a forecast of future fees.

What This Means

For users, Ethereum in fall 2026 is cheaper on both mainnet and, especially, on rollups, which remain the sensible default for everyday activity. For the ecosystem, the pattern confirms a maturing division of labour: rollups handle execution at scale while mainnet provides settlement and security. The open question is revenue sustainability, not usability. We recommend watching the L2 share and data-availability fees alongside mainnet gwei in the coming quarter to judge whether the economics keep pace with the technical progress.
