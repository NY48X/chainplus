Understanding Ethereum: Smart Contracts and DeFi

By ChainPlus Editorial · July 28, 2026 · Explainer

Ethereum is the second-largest cryptocurrency by market cap and the leading smart contract platform. While Bitcoin was designed as digital money, Ethereum was designed as a programmable blockchain — a platform for building decentralized applications (dApps).

What Are Smart Contracts?

A smart contract is a self-executing program stored on the blockchain. It automatically enforces the terms of an agreement when predefined conditions are met. No intermediary is needed — the code itself is the enforcement mechanism.

For example, a simple smart contract might say: "If Alice sends 1 ETH to this contract, and Bob sends 1 ETH, then send 2 ETH to a designated address." The contract executes automatically when both conditions are met.

Smart contracts are written in Solidity (Ethereum's primary programming language) and deployed to the Ethereum blockchain. Once deployed, they cannot be altered — the code is immutable. This is both a feature (trustless execution) and a risk (bugs can't be patched easily).

Decentralized Finance (DeFi)

DeFi is a financial system built on smart contracts, primarily on Ethereum. It includes:

  • Decentralized exchanges (DEXs): Trade tokens without a centralized exchange. Uniswap and SushiSwap are the most popular.
  • Lending protocols: Lend or borrow crypto assets. Aave and Compound are leading examples.
  • Stablecoins: Cryptocurrencies pegged to fiat currencies. DAI is a decentralized stablecoin backed by crypto collateral.
  • Yield farming: Earn returns by providing liquidity to DeFi protocols.
  • Derivatives: Trade synthetic assets and perpetual futures on-chain.

DeFi's key advantage is composability — smart contracts can interact with each other like Lego blocks. You can take a loan from Aave, trade on Uniswap, and deposit into a yield farm, all in a single transaction.

Ethereum vs. Bitcoin

Bitcoin and Ethereum serve different purposes. Bitcoin is digital gold — a store of value with a fixed supply. Ethereum is a programmable platform — a computer that runs decentralized applications.

Ethereum has no supply cap, though its issuance rate has decreased significantly after the "Merge" (transition to proof-of-stake in 2022). The key metric is not total supply but the burn rate — Ethereum burns a portion of transaction fees, making it potentially deflationary during periods of high usage.

The Scaling Challenge

Ethereum's main limitation is scalability. The base layer (Layer 1) processes roughly 15-30 transactions per second — far below what's needed for mainstream adoption. High demand leads to high gas fees (transaction costs), which can exceed $50 during peak periods.

The solution is Layer 2 scaling — networks built on top of Ethereum that process transactions off-chain and post compressed data back to Ethereum for security. Arbitrum, Optimism, Base, and zkSync are the leading Layer 2 networks.

Why Ethereum Matters

Ethereum is the foundation of the decentralized web. Most DeFi protocols, NFT marketplaces, and decentralized applications are built on Ethereum or Ethereum-compatible chains. Understanding Ethereum is essential for anyone who wants to participate in the crypto ecosystem beyond simple buying and selling.

Ethereum's Tokenomics

Ethereum's monetary policy changed significantly after the Merge. Previously, new ETH was issued to miners as block rewards. Now, validators receive smaller rewards, and a portion of transaction fees is burned (destroyed).

This means Ethereum's supply can decrease during periods of high network usage. The "ultrasound money" thesis argues that ETH could become deflationary over time, making it a better store of value than Bitcoin. This is debated but represents a significant shift in Ethereum's economic model.

The Ethereum Ecosystem

Ethereum is more than a blockchain — it's an ecosystem. Thousands of projects are built on Ethereum or Ethereum-compatible chains. This includes DeFi protocols (Uniswap, Aave, MakerDAO), NFT marketplaces (OpenSea), Layer 2 networks (Arbitrum, Optimism), and infrastructure (Chainlink, The Graph).

This ecosystem effect is Ethereum's strongest competitive advantage. Even if other blockchains offer better technical performance, Ethereum's network effects and developer community make it the default platform for new projects.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Ethereum a good investment?

Ethereum is the leading smart contract platform with a large ecosystem and strong developer community. Like all crypto assets, it carries significant risk. Its value depends on continued adoption of Ethereum-based applications and the success of its scaling roadmap.

How is Ethereum different from Bitcoin?

Bitcoin is designed as digital money and a store of value. Ethereum is designed as a programmable platform for decentralized applications. Bitcoin has a fixed supply; Ethereum's supply is dynamic. Bitcoin uses proof-of-work; Ethereum uses proof-of-stake.

What is gas on Ethereum?

Gas is the fee paid to execute transactions on Ethereum. It's measured in gwei (a fraction of ETH). Gas fees vary based on network demand — higher demand means higher fees. Layer 2 networks offer much lower gas fees.

Can Ethereum scale to handle millions of users?

Ethereum's base layer cannot. But Layer 2 networks are designed to scale Ethereum to millions of transactions per second. The combination of L1 security and L2 scalability is Ethereum's scaling strategy.
