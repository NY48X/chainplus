Understanding Ethereum: Smart Contracts and DeFi

By Mike Tembo · August 21, 2026 · Explainer

Ethereum is often described as "programmable money", but a more useful description is that it is a shared computer anyone can use. Where Bitcoin lets you move value from one wallet to another, Ethereum lets you run programs — financial contracts, games, marketplaces — with the same guarantee that the results cannot be quietly rewritten.

The short version — in plain English

  • What it is: A blockchain that runs smart contracts: small programs stored on the chain that execute automatically when you interact with them. Those programs power DeFi, NFTs and thousands of decentralised apps.
  • Why people build on it: It was the first general-purpose smart contract platform, so the developers, tools and users accumulated there. That network effect is hard to copy.
  • The trade-off: Popularity makes it expensive — thousands of people compete for limited block space, so fees can spike. Faster, cheaper networks called Layer 2s now handle most everyday activity.

Smart Contracts, Without the Jargon

A smart contract is a program whose code lives on the blockchain. Once deployed, anyone can call it, and the network will run it exactly as written. There is no help desk to reverse a mistake and no manager to approve a request — the code is the procedure.

A simple example makes it concrete: a contract that holds funds until two parties both deposit, then releases the total to an agreed address. A bank would need account holders, compliance checks and a dispute process. The contract needs none of that. The cost is rigidity: if the code has a bug, it will faithfully execute the bug, which is why Ethereum's history includes painful lessons in auditing.

Most smart contracts are written in Solidity. Users interact with them through wallets. When you "swap tokens" on a decentralised exchange or "lend" through a lending pool, you are calling a contract, paying a fee called gas, and receiving a result that the whole network agrees on.

Decentralised Finance (DeFi) on Ethereum

DeFi is the collection of financial applications built from smart contracts. The main pieces are decentralised exchanges (Uniswap, Curve) where tokens trade directly between wallets, lending markets (Aave, Compound) where you deposit collateral to borrow or supply assets to earn interest, and stablecoins that act as the system's dollars.

The important property is composability — contracts can call each other like building blocks. One transaction can borrow from a lending market, swap the result on an exchange and deposit it into another protocol. That Legolike quality is unique to Ethereum and is why so much economic activity clusters there.

How We Got Here: A Short History With Dates

Ethereum's history is a sequence of ambitious ideas followed by hard technical work, punctuated by a few crises that changed its architecture permanently.

December 2013 – July 2014 — the idea and the funding. Vitalik Buterin published the Ethereum whitepaper in December 2013, proposing a blockchain that could run arbitrary programs, not just payments. The project raised funds through a crowdsale in July 2014, settling on a launch timeline for the following year.

30 July 2015 — Frontier. Ethereum went live with the first version of its software and the first ethers (ETH). The network was slow and the tools were rough, but the core innovation — a Turing-complete virtual machine (the EVM) shared by everyone — was live.

June 2016 — the DAO hack. A re-entrancy bug in a popular contract called The DAO let an attacker drain about $50 million worth of ETH. The community split the chain in a controversial hard fork to reverse the theft, creating Ethereum Classic as a result. The incident taught the ecosystem that code immutability collides with human error, and auditing became a profession.

2017 — the ICO boom. The ERC-20 token standard turned token creation into a few lines of code. Thousands of tokens launched, fees spiked, and the platform's scalability limits became obvious. The boom collapsed, but the developer base stayed.

1 December 2020 — the Beacon Chain. Ethereum launched proof of stake on a parallel chain, beginning a multi-year migration that would make the network far more energy efficient.

15 September 2022 — the Merge. Ethereum switched from mining to staking, cutting its energy use by about 99.9% overnight without stopping the network. That was one of the largest coordinated upgrades in computing history.

13 March 2024 — Dencun. The upgrade introduced "blobs", a way for Layer 2 networks to post data to Ethereum cheaply. Layer 2 fees fell by an order of magnitude, and the rollup era became practical for mainstream use.

Ethereum vs. Bitcoin, Side by Side

The comparison that matters is purpose. Bitcoin asks: can we make digital money with absolute scarcity? Ethereum asks: can we make a computer that runs programs no one can shut down or censor? They need different designs, so they make different compromises.

PurposeDigital money / store of valueProgrammable world computer
ConsensusProof of work (mining)Proof of stake (staking)
Block time~10 minutes~12 seconds
SupplyHard cap: 21 millionNo hard cap; issuance < burn at high usage
ProgrammabilityScripts, limitedFull smart contracts (EVM)
Typical feeLow, but slow confirmationVariable; cheap on Layer 2s

Ethereum's monetary policy changed with the Merge. New ETH is now issued to stakers at a much lower rate, and a portion of every transaction fee is burned — destroyed. During busy periods, more is burned than issued, making supply fall. That deflationary dynamic is still debated, but it links ETH's value to usage of the platform itself.

The Scaling Challenge and the Layer 2 Answer

Ethereum's base layer processes roughly 15–30 transactions per second — far below what mainstream applications need. When demand peaks, fees rise until only the most urgent transactions get through. For years that looked like a fatal flaw.

The answer chosen is a modular design: keep Layer 1 as a secure settlement layer, and move execution to Layer 2 networks (Arbitrum, Optimism, Base, zkSync) that process thousands of transactions cheaply and settle back to Ethereum. The Dencun upgrade in March 2024 made that model economically viable at scale.

Why Ethereum Matters

Ethereum is where the experiments that define the next decade of finance are happening first. Tokenised assets, on-chain markets, identity systems and developer tooling all start there because the community is there. Even when applications eventually live elsewhere, they are usually designed to be compatible with Ethereum.

For the rest of us, Ethereum matters because it demonstrates both the promise and the difficulty of programmable money. It works in production, at scale, with real money moving through it daily — and it still struggles with fees, complexity and security failures. That combination is exactly why people keep building on it: the problems are unsolved, but the platform to solve them is open to anyone.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is gas on Ethereum?

Gas is the fee you pay to use the network, priced in gwei (billionths of ETH). Every contract call costs gas, calculated from how much computation it needs. Fees rise when the network is busy. Most users now pay gas on a Layer 2 network where the same transaction costs cents instead of dollars.

How is Ethereum different from Bitcoin?

Bitcoin is designed to be simple, scarce money. Ethereum is designed to be a programmable platform for applications. Bitcoin uses mining; Ethereum uses staking. Bitcoin caps supply at 21 million; Ethereum has no cap but can become deflationary when usage is high.

Is Ethereum a good investment?

It is the leading smart contract platform with the largest developer ecosystem, which gives it the strongest network effect in crypto. Its price is also highly volatile and its value depends on continued adoption of applications built on it. That makes it a high-risk, high-upside asset rather than a safe store of value.

Can Ethereum really scale to millions of users?

The base layer alone cannot, and was never intended to. The scaling strategy is Layer 2 networks settling to Ethereum. With dozens of Layer 2s live and blob data reducing their costs, the combined network can process far more transactions than the base layer alone, though users still face fragmentation between chains.
