What Is Bitcoin and How Does It Work?

By ChainPlus Editorial · July 15, 2026 · Explainer

Bitcoin is the first and largest cryptocurrency, created in 2009 by an anonymous person or group using the name Satoshi Nakamoto. It is a decentralized digital currency that operates without a central bank or single administrator. Transactions are verified by a distributed network of computers and recorded on a public ledger called the blockchain.

How Bitcoin Works

Bitcoin operates on a peer-to-peer network. When you send Bitcoin to someone, the transaction is broadcast to the network, where miners (specialized computers) verify it by solving complex mathematical problems. Once verified, the transaction is added to a "block" and permanently recorded on the blockchain.

The blockchain is a chain of blocks, each containing a batch of transactions. Each block references the previous one, creating an immutable record. This means no transaction can be altered or reversed once confirmed.

The 21 Million Cap

Bitcoin has a hard supply cap of 21 million coins. This is coded into the protocol and cannot be changed without consensus from the entire network. New Bitcoin is created through mining, but the rate of creation halves approximately every four years (the "halving"). This makes Bitcoin deflationary over time — unlike fiat currencies, which can be printed without limit.

Why Bitcoin Matters

Bitcoin introduced the concept of digital scarcity. Before Bitcoin, digital files could be copied infinitely. Bitcoin solved the "double-spend problem" — ensuring that digital money can't be spent twice — without requiring a trusted central authority.

Bitcoin is often compared to "digital gold" because of its fixed supply and store-of-value properties. It is also used as a medium of exchange, though its price volatility makes it less practical for everyday transactions than stablecoins or traditional currencies.

Common Misconceptions

Bitcoin is anonymous. Not exactly. Bitcoin transactions are pseudonymous — they're linked to wallet addresses, not names. But with enough analysis, transactions can often be traced to individuals. For true anonymity, privacy coins like Monero are designed differently.

Bitcoin is only used for illegal activity. Studies consistently show that the vast majority of Bitcoin transactions are for legitimate purposes. The transparent nature of the blockchain actually makes it easier to trace illicit activity than cash.

Bitcoin has no intrinsic value. This is a philosophical question. Bitcoin's value comes from network effects (more users = more valuable), scarcity (fixed supply), and utility (store of value, transfer mechanism). Whether that constitutes "intrinsic value" depends on your definition.

How to Think About Bitcoin

Bitcoin is best understood as an experiment in decentralized money. It has survived for over 15 years, weathered multiple crashes, and gained institutional adoption. Whether it succeeds long-term depends on continued adoption, regulatory treatment, and technological development.

For anyone interested in crypto, understanding Bitcoin is essential. It is the foundation of the entire cryptocurrency ecosystem — most other cryptocurrencies are either forks of Bitcoin or built in response to its limitations.

Bitcoin's Environmental Impact

Bitcoin mining consumes significant energy. The proof-of-work consensus mechanism requires miners to solve complex mathematical problems, which demands substantial computational power. Critics argue this is wasteful and environmentally harmful.

Proponents counter that much of Bitcoin mining uses renewable energy (hydro, solar, wind) and that the energy consumption is justified by the security it provides. The debate is ongoing, with both sides presenting valid points.

It's worth noting that traditional banking and gold mining also consume significant energy. The comparison depends on which metrics you prioritize and how you account for externalities.

Bitcoin and Regulation

Bitcoin's regulatory status varies by country. Some nations (El Salvador) have adopted it as legal tender. Others (China) have banned it. Most countries treat it as property for tax purposes.

Regulatory clarity is improving but remains fragmented. The US is developing frameworks for crypto taxation, custody, and trading. The EU's MiCA regulation provides a comprehensive framework for crypto assets. These developments will shape Bitcoin's future adoption and use.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Bitcoin a good investment?

Bitcoin has historically been a high-risk, high-reward asset. It has delivered significant returns over long periods but with extreme volatility. Whether it's a good investment depends on your risk tolerance, time horizon, and portfolio diversification. Never invest more than you can afford to lose.

How do I buy Bitcoin?

You can buy Bitcoin on cryptocurrency exchanges like Coinbase, Kraken, or Binance. You'll need to create an account, verify your identity, and deposit funds. You can also buy Bitcoin through Bitcoin ATMs or peer-to-peer platforms.

Can Bitcoin be hacked?

The Bitcoin network itself has never been hacked. However, exchanges, wallets, and individual users can be compromised. The security of your Bitcoin depends on how you store it. Hardware wallets are the most secure option for significant holdings.

What happens when all 21 million Bitcoin are mined?

The last Bitcoin is expected to be mined around 2140. After that, miners will be compensated solely by transaction fees. The network is designed to transition smoothly to a fee-based security model.
