What Is Bitcoin and How Does It Work?

By Mike Tembo · August 14, 2026 · Explainer

Bitcoin is often introduced as "digital money" and then left as a mystery. The better starting point is simpler: it is a piece of software that lets strangers transfer value without trusting each other or a bank in the middle, and it is also the name of the asset that runs on that software. Those two ideas — the network and the coin — are related but not identical.

The short version — in plain English

  • What it is: A public, open-source network that records every Bitcoin transaction on a shared ledger called the blockchain. No single company runs it and no government can shut it down in one place.
  • Why it has a price: There will only ever be 21 million bitcoin. As more people want to hold it, that fixed supply is what gives the market a price — the same way gold is valuable partly because you cannot print more of it easily.
  • How it stays honest: A process called mining. Computers compete to confirm new batches of transactions about every ten minutes, and the winner is paid in newly created bitcoin.

How Bitcoin Works

Imagine a notebook that everyone in the world can read but nobody can rewrite. That is the Bitcoin blockchain. Every transaction — who sent how much to whom — is written into that notebook, and once a page is closed, it cannot be altered without redoing all the work that came after it. That makes the history of payments tamper-evident by design.

To send bitcoin, your wallet signs a message with your private key, which is a secret only you hold. The network checks that the signature matches the coins you claim to own and that you have not already spent them — the famous "double-spend" problem that stopped digital money from working before. If the checks pass, miners include the transaction in the next block.

Mining uses proof of work. Miners run a specialised search — trying billions of combinations of numbers until one produces a hash (a digital fingerprint) below a difficulty target set by the network. The first to find it broadcasts the block, and the rest of the network verifies the proof almost instantly. The puzzle is hard to solve but trivial to check, which is the entire trick.

Every 210,000 blocks, roughly every four years, the reward for mining is cut in half. This is called the halving. It means the supply schedule is predictable down to the last coin: the final bitcoin is expected around the year 2140, after which miners will be paid only in transaction fees.

How We Got Here: A Short History With Dates

Bitcoin did not appear overnight, and it did not succeed in a straight line. Its history explains why the system behaves the way it does today — conservative, slow to change, and built to survive being attacked.

31 October 2008 — the whitepaper. A pseudonymous author calling himself Satoshi Nakamoto published "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System" on a cryptography mailing list. It proposed a payment network with no central authority, using cryptography and proof of work to solve double-spending.

3 January 2009 — the genesis block. Nakamoto mined the first block, block 0, and embedded a headline from The Times: "Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks." It was a timestamp and a statement of intent — money that could not be inflated away by bank rescues.

22 May 2010 — Bitcoin Pizza Day. A user in Florida paid 10,000 bitcoin for two pizzas. At the time that was a fair market price. It is now used as a reminder of how dramatically value has compound in the early network. No one knows what happened to Nakamoto — the protocol has run for over 17 years without a founder to change it.

2011 – 2013 — exchanges and the first boom. The Mt. Gox exchange brought trading to the public, the price crossed $1 in 2011, and the first major bubble and crash followed in 2013. Regulators began to pay attention, and the term "cryptocurrency" entered the press.

28 November 2012, 9 July 2016, 11 May 2020, 19 April 2024 — the four halvings. The mining reward fell from 50 to 25, then to 12.5, 6.25 and finally 3.125 BTC per block at block 840,000 in April 2024. Each halving halves the default inflation of the system, trending to the fixed 21 million cap.

January 2024 — US spot ETFs. The US Securities and Exchange Commission approved spot Bitcoin exchange-traded funds, letting investors hold bitcoin through a brokerage account without touching an exchange or a wallet. That was the largest institutional door-opening in the asset's history.

The 21 Million Cap and What It Really Means

Only 21 million bitcoin will ever exist. That is not a marketing claim; it is a rule in the software that every full node enforces. Changing it would require persuading the whole network to run different software, which the economic majority has never agreed to do. Scarcity, in Bitcoin's case, is enforced by code and verified by anyone who wants to check.

Scarcity alone, however, does not create value. A rare token nobody wants is worth nothing. Bitcoin's price comes from the intersection of fixed supply and sustained demand — from people who value censorship resistance, from savers who want an asset outside the banking system, and from speculators who expect both groups to grow. Understanding that two-part equation keeps you from attributing every price move to a single cause.

Of the 21 million, about 19.8 million had been mined by early 2026. The remaining coins will trickle out slowly over more than a century, which means the inflation rate is effectively under one percent a year and falling. That is why Bitcoin is often compared, carefully, to digital gold: both are scarce, durable and portable, but gold has thousands of years of cultural weight behind it and Bitcoin has about seventeen.

Why Bitcoin Matters

Bitcoin proved that digital scarcity is possible without a central issuer. That sounds abstract, but it is a genuinely new primitive in economics. Before Bitcoin, every digital thing could be copied perfectly and infinitely. Bitcoin showed how to create a digital object that cannot be copied and double-spent, while being transferable across borders in minutes.

For people living under capital controls, inflation or sanctions, that transferability is not theoretical. For everyone else, the lesson is institutional: you can build a monetary network that no single party can freeze, seize or re-write, at the cost of slower transactions and more volatile prices. Whether that trade is worth it depends on what you value — which is why Bitcoin has both passionate advocates and legitimate sceptics.

It also matters well beyond its own price. Every major cryptocurrency since 2009 has been a response to Bitcoin's limitations — faster blocks, programmable contracts, privacy, or scale. You cannot understand the rest of the ecosystem without understanding the system it was all built to improve on, or to escape from.

Common Misconceptions, Straightened Out

"Bitcoin is anonymous." It is pseudonymous. Addresses are not labelled with names, but every transaction is public forever. Investigators routinely trace funds through that public trail. If anonymity is the goal, tools designed for privacy are a different conversation.

"Bitcoin is mostly for crime." The transparent ledger is actively hostile to large-scale crime compared with cash, and studies of on-chain flows have consistently found that illicit activity is a small minority of volume. Small retail payments should not be conflated with the network's main use.

"Bitcoin will replace the dollar next year." There is no timetable like that in the protocol. Adoption has been gradual, cycle-driven and reversible, and Bitcoin competes with — rather than simply replaces — existing monetary systems.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Bitcoin a good investment?

It has been among the highest-returning assets over multi-year periods and among the most volatile, with drawdowns of 70% or more in past cycles. That makes it a high-risk asset that is unsuitable as a core holding for most people. Anyone considering it should treat it as speculation, size it small, and never invest money they cannot afford to lose.

How do I buy bitcoin safely?

Most people start on a regulated exchange, verify their identity, buy, and then withdraw to a self-custody wallet for larger amounts. For meaningful holdings, a hardware wallet with a securely backed-up recovery phrase is the standard. The exchange is the place most hacks happen — the Bitcoin network itself has never been hacked.

What happens when all 21 million are mined?

Mining rewards will then come only from transaction fees. The network was designed with that transition in mind, and fee markets have grown as usage has increased. Whether fees alone will be sufficient to secure the network in 2140 is an open research question that current fee levels do not yet settle.

Is Bitcoin anonymous and untraceable?

No. Transactions are permanent and public. Privacy can be improved with techniques like CoinJoin, but the base layer is an open ledger by design.
