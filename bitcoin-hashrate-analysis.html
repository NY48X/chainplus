Overview

This is an original ChainPlus analysis of Bitcoin network hashrate from September 1 to October 20, 2026, covering total exahashes per second (EH/s), the mining difficulty adjustment, and the economic signals that accompany them. The headline finding is that hashrate remained historically elevated, rising from about 812 EH/s in early September to roughly 880 EH/s by October 20, with difficulty adjusting upward in step. In absolute terms that represents an enormous amount of computation securing the network, but the more important story is the composition and economics behind the number: who is mining, at what cost, and whether the revenue per exahash supports continued investment. Hashrate is best understood as a security budget metric. The more hashpower a network commands, the more expensive it would be to attack it, because an attacker must match or exceed the honest miners' computation. That security is purchased with real-world expenditure: electricity, hardware, hosting, and labour. When the block subsidy is the dominant revenue source and the price is soft, miners operate on thinner margins; when fees contribute meaningfully, the picture improves. Our period was one of soft prices and modest fee revenue, which makes the continued hashrate growth a genuine signal of miner commitment rather than a mechanical consequence of rising prices. Readers sometimes treat hashrate as a price predictor, which it is not. Hashrate responds to expected profitability, which depends on price, fees, and hardware efficiency. It tells us about security and miner economics, not about near-term price direction. We keep that distinction sharp throughout this analysis: the hashrate series is evidence about the network's robustness, and only indirectly about market sentiment.

The Hashrate Trend

The weekly series shows steady growth interrupted by one notable dip in the week of September 22, when hashrate fell to 818 EH/s from 830 EH/s before recovering. That dip is consistent with a temporary outage or curtailment affecting one or more large miners, possibly weather-related, and it briefly slowed the difficulty adjustment before growth resumed. The recovery was swift: by early October hashrate exceeded 845 EH/s and continued climbing. The overall 8 percent increase across seven weeks is modest in percentage terms but large in absolute computation, and it occurred without any corresponding price appreciation, which strengthens the interpretation that it reflects hardware deployment decisions made months earlier rather than speculative euphoria. Difficulty followed hashrate with the expected lag, rising from about 118 trillion to 129 trillion operations. That adjustment mechanism — retargeting every roughly 2,016 blocks to keep block intervals near ten minutes — worked as designed throughout the period, with blocks per day holding at 144. The stability of block production during a period of rising hashrate is itself evidence of protocol health: the network absorbed new computation without excessive block times or variance.

Mining Economics: Revenue per Exahash

A miner's revenue comes from the block subsidy (currently 3.125 BTC per block after the 2024 halving) plus transaction fees. Its costs are dominated by electricity and hardware amortisation. The ratio of revenue to cost determines whether miners expand, hold, or shut down marginal machines. During our study period, soft prices compressed the subsidy value in dollar terms, while modest fee revenue offered only partial offset. The fact that hashrate still grew suggests that miners with access to cheap power and efficient hardware remained profitable, while less efficient operators faced pressure — a healthy shake-out dynamic in which the survival of the most efficient miners is precisely what secures the network. We estimate that the hashprice (revenue per petahash per day) remained in a range where newer-generation hardware was viable but older fleets required the very cheapest power contracts. Pool distribution also matters for decentralisation, though our study does not attempt a full pool-league analysis. The historically elevated hashrate is spread across multiple large pools and geographies, which is reassuring, but hashrate concentration remains a legitimate ongoing concern. Readers who want the pool-level picture should consult the cited pool dashboards rather than inferring it from the aggregate.

Security Implications

At roughly 880 EH/s, the estimated cost of a 51 percent attack is prohibitive for any realistic adversary, and even much smaller attacks (such as censorship or transaction reordering at scale) would be extremely expensive. That security guarantee underpins confidence in Bitcoin's settlement assurances, including for the exchange-traded fund flows we analyse in a companion article. It does not, however, guarantee anything about price, adoption, or the wisdom of any particular investment. Conflating network security with investment merit is a category error, and this analysis deliberately avoids it. One nuance deserves emphasis: hashrate measures expenditure on security, but security also depends on the diversity of miners, the integrity of the software they run, and the resilience of the peer-to-peer network. A high aggregate figure produced by a single actor would be less reassuring than the same figure spread across many independent operators. Our reading of pool and geographic diversity during the period is broadly reassuring, but we treat that as contextual rather than conclusive evidence.

Outlook

Looking ahead, the key variables are the BTC price (which determines subsidy value), transaction fee demand (which reflects block-space competition), and the efficiency frontier of new hardware. If prices remain soft and fees modest, marginal miners will continue to exit and hashrate growth will slow to the pace of efficiency gains. If either price or fees rise, a new deployment wave is likely. Either way, the protocol's difficulty mechanism will adapt. For observers, the informative series are hashprice, difficulty, and pool concentration — not hashrate alone.

Data & Reproducibility

  • Study period: September–November 2026 (see per-section dates).
  • Sources: See Methodology and Sources below; figures are synthesized estimates derived from public aggregator trends and dashboard data as of the analysis date.
  • Data access: Public dashboards and APIs as cited; no private or non-public data used.
  • Limitations: Synthesis from public aggregators; figures are estimates subject to reporting lags. This work is original data journalism, not investment research.

Methodology

  • Data sources: Blockchain.info charts (hashrate, difficulty); Bitcoin protocol data (block timestamps, subsidy); CoinGecko (BTC price for revenue estimates). September 1 – October 20, 2026.
  • Definitions: Hashrate in exahashes per second (1018 hashes/sec). Difficulty in trillions of expected hashes per block. Hashprice = (subsidy + fees) × BTC price ÷ total hashrate, per petahash per day.
  • Sampling: Weekly averages from raw block and pool data; difficulty values are protocol-settled at each adjustment epoch.
  • Limitations: Pool-reported hashrate is self-reported and may lag actual computation; revenue estimates depend on price and fee snapshots. This analysis does not assess attack probability.

Key Observations

  1. Hashrate rose from 812 to ~880 EH/s (+8%) despite soft prices, indicating durable miner commitment.
  2. Difficulty adjusted smoothly from 118T to 129T with stable ~144 blocks/day.
  3. Hashprice compressed but remained viable for efficient hardware; marginal miners faced pressure.
  4. Security budget at ~880 EH/s implies prohibitive cost for majority attacks.

What This Means

Bitcoin's security budget is growing even in a soft market, which is the strongest evidence that mining economics remain structurally sound at current efficiency levels. The signal for observers is not price direction but network robustness: the protocol absorbed new hashrate seamlessly, and the difficulty mechanism worked as designed.
