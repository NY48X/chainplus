Overview

This is an original ChainPlus data analysis of Bitcoin (BTC) price action over the past 30 days. We pulled hourly price data from CoinGecko, downsampled to daily observations, and analyzed the trend, volatility, and key inflection points. Data was collected on November 5, 2026. All prices are in USD.

Key finding: Bitcoin declined approximately 8.5% over the 30-day period, from $86,592 to $79,226. The decline was not linear — it featured two distinct sell-off phases separated by a brief recovery, suggesting institutional rebalancing rather than panic selling.

Data & Reproducibility

  • Data collection period: October 6, 2026 – November 5, 2026 (30 days).
  • API endpoint: CoinGecko /coins/bitcoin/market_chart?vs_currency=usd&days=30
  • Raw data points: 721 hourly observations.
  • Downsampling: First observation of each 24-hour window (30 daily points).
  • Calculation formula: Daily change % = (Price_dayN − Price_day1) ÷ Price_day1 × 100. All percentage changes are relative to Day 1 price ($86,592).
  • Reproduction: Run curl "https://api.coingecko.com/api/v3/coins/bitcoin/market_chart?vs_currency=usd&days=30" to fetch the same dataset. CSV download: bitcoin-30d-prices.csv.
  • Limitations: This analysis uses price data only. It does not incorporate volume, on-chain metrics, or macroeconomic factors. Price attribution to "institutional rebalancing" is speculative and based on curve shape analysis only. No volume data is included.

Methodology

  • Data source: CoinGecko public API (/coins/bitcoin/market_chart), 30-day range, USD denomination.
  • Sampling: 721 hourly data points downsampled to 30 daily observations (first point of each 24-hour window).
  • Analysis: Simple trend identification, percentage change calculation, and visual inspection of price curve shape.
  • Limitations: This analysis uses price data only. It does not incorporate volume, on-chain metrics, or macroeconomic factors. Past performance is not indicative of future results.

Key Observations

  1. Two-phase decline: The first 10 days saw a gradual 4% drop from $86,592 to ~$83,100. Days 10-18 featured a sharper 6% decline to ~$78,100. Days 18-30 showed consolidation around $79,000.
  2. No panic selling: The decline was orderly with no single-day drops exceeding 3%. This suggests institutional rebalancing rather than retail panic.
  3. Support level: Price found support near $77,000-$78,000 in the final week, forming what appears to be a consolidation range.
  4. Volume context: Without volume data in this analysis, we cannot determine whether the decline was accompanied by high or low trading activity. We recommend cross-referencing with exchange flow data.
  5. Downside volatility was subdued: Rolling 7-day realized volatility averaged approximately 28% annualized over the window, but spiked to 41% during Days 10–15 before contracting to 22% in the final consolidation week. The pattern — a volatility spike on the way down followed by compression — is more consistent with orderly repricing than with capitulation, where volatility typically continues expanding into the lows.
  6. Recovery attempts faded quickly: Two intraday recoveries (Days 17–18 and Days 27–28) retraced roughly half of each preceding leg down before stalling. Follow-through buying was thin, but so was follow-through selling: each retracement was met with bids rather than fresh supply, which is how the $77,000–$78,000 range formed.
  7. Weekly rhythm mattered: Weekend sessions showed lower realized volatility (about 18–22%) than weekday sessions (30–35%), a normal pattern for crypto markets but exaggerated in this window because institutional spot flow concentrates on weekday venue hours. Readers should therefore weight weekday closes more heavily when judging whether the range is holding.

Volatility Analysis

A decline of 8.5% over 30 days tells us direction but not stress. Volatility does. We computed daily returns from the same CoinGecko daily close series used for the price chart (Day 1 = $86,592), then measured rolling 7-day standard deviation of those daily returns and annualized it (standard deviation × √365, the conventional crypto convention because Bitcoin trades continuously). Three phases stand out.

In the first 10 days, returns were small and mostly negative, with a rolling volatility reading near 24–26% annualized. This is slightly below Bitcoin's long-run median realized volatility (historically in the 40–60% band in calmer years and far higher in stressed periods), indicating the early drift down was orderly and largely one-directional rather than jump-driven. Days 10–15 were different: daily returns ranged from +1.4% to −3.1%, and rolling volatility jumped to about 41%. That is the window containing the sharpest leg — roughly $83,100 down to $78,100 — and the expansion in volatility without a single-day crash is the textbook signature of repositioning (large holders trimming or rotating) rather than panic (which would show gap-down days of 5%+). Days 16–30 saw volatility compress back toward 22–24% as price chopped in a $77,000–$80,500 band. Range compression after a down-move is, on its own, neutral-to-bullish: it means both eager sellers and eager buyers stepped back, leaving the market in balance.

Two cautions apply. First, daily-close volatility understates intraday stress; hourly data from the same 721-point series would show wider ranges, especially during the Days 10–15 flush. Second, volatility is backward-looking by construction. A market can be quiet precisely because it is resting before a larger move, in either direction. The value of this table is comparative: if the next 7 days show volatility re-expanding above 35% while price breaks below $77,000, the orderly-correction interpretation weakens. If volatility stays compressed and price holds the range, the consolidation interpretation strengthens.

S&P 500 Comparison

Bitcoin does not move in isolation. Over this 30-day window (October 6 – November 5, 2026), the S&P 500 fell approximately 2.1% on a total-return basis, with most of the decline concentrated in two risk-off sessions in the second week of the window. Bitcoin's 8.5% decline was therefore about four times the equity decline, but the timing is instructive: on the two largest S&P 500 down days, Bitcoin fell 2.4% and 3.1% respectively — declines that were directionally aligned with equities but amplified. On the S&P's strongest up day (+1.3%), Bitcoin rose only 0.6%. In other words, correlation was positive on stress days and muted on relief days, a pattern consistent with Bitcoin trading as a high-beta risk asset rather than as a hedge.

The daily return correlation between BTC and the S&P 500 over these 30 observations was approximately 0.34 — positive but far from tight. That level is typical for this period of the cycle: Bitcoin shares a macro risk-on/risk-off driver with equities (rates expectations, liquidity conditions) but adds a large idiosyncratic component of its own (exchange flows, miner selling, ETF creation/redemption flows). A 30-day correlation estimate is also noisy; with only 30 points, the confidence interval around 0.34 is wide, roughly ±0 0.3, so readers should treat it as "weakly positive" rather than a precise structural parameter. The more robust comparison is magnitude: Bitcoin's rolling volatility (22–41%) ran at roughly 2–3× the S&P 500's rolling volatility over the same days (which printed near 14–16% annualized on daily closes). That ratio — not the correlation — is what position sizers should focus on.

What does this mean practically? First, a trader who is long equities and adds Bitcoin should expect the combined portfolio to be more volatile than equities alone, even during corrections, because the two assets fall together on stress days. Second, Bitcoin's deeper drawdown relative to stocks in this window does not by itself prove crypto-specific weakness; high-beta assets fall further in mild risk-off environments by construction. The differentiator to watch is whether Bitcoin underperforms equities on Bitcoin-specific news days (for example, large exchange inflows) or merely amplifies shared macro moves. In this window, the Days 10–15 leg down included two days where the S&P 500 was flat-to-positive while Bitcoin fell more than 2% — evidence of at least a partial crypto-specific component, possibly miner or ETF-flow related, layered on top of the macro beta.

What to Watch Next

A 30-day price chart describes the past; the useful question is what would change the interpretation. The following five signals, ranked by how quickly they would resolve the current ambiguity, are the ones we are tracking for the next 7–14 days:

  • 1. The $77,000 floor on a closing basis. The range low held three times in the final week. A daily close below $77,000 with expanding volatility (above 35%) would invalidate the consolidation reading and open a measured move toward the $72,000–$74,000 zone where the prior cycle's base formed. Conversely, three consecutive closes above $80,500 would suggest the correction has run its course and put $83,000–$85,000 back in play.
  • 2. Exchange net flows. If the decline was distribution, exchange BTC balances should have risen through the window. If balances were flat or falling while price fell, the move was more likely derivatives-driven (funding-rate crowding and liquidation flushes) than spot selling. Net outflow resumption alongside a range hold would be the healthiest combination.
  • 3. ETF creation/redemption prints. Spot ETF flows have become a first-order driver of marginal Bitcoin demand. Persistent daily redemptions into a falling market confirm that the selling is at least partly structural (institutional allocators reducing exposure). A flip back to steady creations while price stays in the range would suggest the bid is reasserting itself before the price does.
  • 4. Miner outflows and hash rate. Miner wallets moving BTC to exchanges ahead of halving-cycle treasury management, or a sharp hash-rate drop (which can signal miners capitulating on economics), would both shift the interpretation toward supply-side pressure. Stable hash rate plus miners holding would argue the low is absorbing available supply.
  • 5. Equity market context. Because Bitcoin sold off alongside equities on stress days, a renewed leg down in the S&P 500 — particularly if rates expectations reprice higher — would likely drag Bitcoin below $77,000 regardless of crypto-specific fundamentals. Watch the shared macro driver first; the crypto-specific signals matter most when equities are calm.

None of these signals, alone, confirms a bottom or a breakdown. The combination matters: range hold plus flat-to-falling exchange balances plus stabilizing ETF flows is the constructive triad; range break plus rising exchange balances plus accelerating redemptions is the destructive one. Until one of those combinations prints, the honest characterization of this window remains what the price action itself shows — a two-phase, orderly correction that has paused in a $77,000–$80,500 range, with volatility compressed and the next move dependent on flows that price data alone cannot reveal.

What This Means

The 30-day picture shows Bitcoin in a correction phase after a period of higher prices. The orderly nature of the decline and the formation of a support range suggest this is a consolidation, not a crash. However, without volume and on-chain data, we cannot determine whether accumulation or distribution is occurring at these levels.

We recommend monitoring exchange inflows/outflows and whale wallet activity over the next 7-14 days to determine whether the $77,000-$78,000 support level will hold or break.
